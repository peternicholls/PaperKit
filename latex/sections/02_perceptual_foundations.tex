\section{Perceptual Foundations}
\label{sec:perceptual-foundations}

The design of temporally coherent color progressions rests on a rigorous foundation in perceptual color science. This section establishes three principles that underpin subsequent design decisions: (1) perceptual color difference is well modeled by a Riemannian metric rather than a globally Euclidean geometry; (2) no single global three-dimensional Euclidean coordinate system can be perfectly perceptually uniform without introducing distortion; and (3) temporal color perception differs materially from spatial color perception. These are empirically supported constraints with direct implications for how color journeys should be constructed.

% 2.1 The Riemannian Structure of Color Space
% ------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Color Space as Riemannian Manifold}
\label{sec:riemannian-color}

\subsection{Theoretical Framework}

Recent comprehensive measurements of human color discrimination thresholds provide strong evidence that perceptual color difference is well modeled as a \emph{Riemannian manifold}, a space that is locally Euclidean but globally curved \citep{hong2025}. This has direct implications for color interpolation and distance measurement.

In a Euclidean space, the shortest path between two points is a straight line, and distances can be computed using Pythagorean geometry. In a Riemannian manifold, the shortest path is a \emph{geodesic}, a curve that follows the intrinsic geometry of the space \citep{hong2025}. Perceptual difference can be usefully approximated by the geodesic distance implied by the local discrimination metric, particularly in the small-step regime relevant to smooth transitions \citep{hong2025}.

Hong et al.\ \citeyearpar{hong2025} characterized this structure empirically through approximately 6,000 discrimination threshold trials per participant, mapping elliptical contours of equal perceptual distance across color space.\footnote{Hong et al.'s study employed the Bayesian Wishart Process Psychophysical Model (WPPM) within a Riemannian manifold framework, providing comprehensive characterization of the isoluminant plane through extensive empirical measurement.}

\subsubsection*{The metric tensor}

In practice, discrimination varies with location in color space. This can be modeled by a position-dependent \emph{metric tensor} $g(\mathbf{x})$ that defines local perceptual distance via the line element:

\begin{equation}
ds^2 = d\mathbf{x}^\top g(\mathbf{x})\, d\mathbf{x}
\label{eq:line-element}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathbf{x}$ represents coordinates in color space and $ds$ is the local perceptual distance. Distances along specific directions (e.g., hue at fixed lightness and chroma) follow from the corresponding components of $g$ integrated along a path. Hong et al.'s findings reveal systematic patterns in the metric tensor components:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Radial orientation:} Major axes of threshold ellipses are consistently oriented toward the achromatic center \citep{hong2025}
    \item \textbf{Lightness compression:} Thresholds are smaller along the lightness dimension than along chromatic axes
    \item \textbf{Geodesic curvature:} Perceptually optimal paths between distant colors are curved, not straight, in standard colorimetric coordinates
\end{enumerate}

This Riemannian framework has historical foundations extending to Schrödinger's extension of Helmholtz's line element \citep{roberti2023}.\footnote{Roberti and Peruzzi provide a comprehensive analysis of Schrödinger's (1920) Riemannian color metric and its improvements over Helmholtz's original formulation—critical foundations for understanding perceptual color space geometry and geodesic paths in color applications.} Roberti and Peruzzi \citeyearpar{roberti2023} note that Schrödinger distinguished between \emph{lower color metrics} (where affine geometry suffices for color mixture) and \emph{higher color metrics} (where Riemannian geometry is necessary to translate color differences into perceptual distances). The engine's goals align with the domain of higher color metrics.

\subsection{Practical Implications}

For color journey construction, the Riemannian structure has three critical consequences:

\begin{description}
    \item[Linear interpolation in RGB is perceptually nonuniform] Naïve component-wise interpolation in gamma-encoded sRGB between saturated blue $(0, 0, 255)$ and saturated yellow $(255, 255, 0)$ produces a midpoint near $(127, 127, 127)$, a desaturated grey, because the straight line passes through the achromatic region. The perceptually smooth path would curve around the exterior of color space.
    
    \item[Distance measurements require appropriate metrics] A $\Delta E$ of 10 in a highly saturated region may be perceptually equivalent to a $\Delta E$ of 5 in a near-neutral region if the underlying metric tensor differs. Hong et al.'s ellipse measurements \citep{hong2025} provide the data needed to construct position-dependent distance corrections.

    \item[Gamut mapping must follow geodesics] When projecting out-of-gamut colors to displayable coordinates, the projection should follow perceptual geodesics rather than straight lines in RGB (\S\ref{sec:gamut-correction}). Hong et al.'s finding that discrimination ellipses orient radially toward the achromatic center \citep{hong2025} justifies reducing saturation (moving toward achromatic) as the perceptually minimal modification.
\end{description}

\subsection{Computational Approximation}

True geodesic computation in a Riemannian manifold requires solving differential equations or variational problems, which is computationally expensive for real-time applications. The engine adopts a pragmatic approach: work in a color space where \emph{Euclidean straight lines approximate geodesics sufficiently well}. This is the definition of perceptual uniformity.

\subsubsection*{Caveat on global structure and suprathreshold perception}

While a Riemannian metric is an effective model of \emph{local} discrimination structure, evidence suggests that suprathreshold dissimilarity judgements may be nonadditive (for example exhibiting diminishing returns for large separations) \citep{bujack2022}.\footnote{Bujack et al.~argue that many perceptual color metrics are valid primarily for small differences, with large color differences exhibiting nonadditive behavior under psychophysical testing—a departure from strict Riemannian geometry.} Accordingly, we treat metric-based distance as a practical local approximation for constructing smooth paths with bounded per-step changes, and we present global distance modelling as an open area for further empirical refinement.

Modern perceptually uniform color spaces (OKLab, CAM16-UCS) are designed such that Euclidean distance $\Delta E$ in the coordinate representation approximates geodesic distance in the underlying perceptual manifold \citep{ottosson2020,safdar2017}. The approximation is not perfect, local curvature remains \citep{hong2025}, but the error is small enough for practical interpolation tasks.

The engine thus achieves a balance: acknowledging the Riemannian nature of color space theoretically while exploiting Euclidean approximations computationally. All subsequent constraints and optimization objectives ($\Delta_{\min}$, $\Delta_{\max}$, velocity weights) are interpreted as \emph{locally Euclidean} values in OKLab units and are intended to operate in the small-step regime.

\section{The 720° Topology and Euclidean Impossibility}
\label{sec:topology-impossibility}

\subsection{Mathematical Statement}

A fundamental result in perceptual color theory is that no single global 3D Euclidean coordinate system can make perceptual distance everywhere equal to Euclidean distance without distortion \citep[p.~2]{nolle2013h2si_ms}. Consequently, practical 3D color spaces must either accept nonuniformity or treat distance as position-dependent (via a metric tensor). This is not merely a limitation of current measurement techniques; it reflects a mathematical constraint closely related to the ``super-importance of hue'' first identified by Judd \citep[p.~11]{judd1940}.\footnote{Judd's concept of the ``super-importance of hue'' established that hue differences contribute disproportionately to perceived color difference in ways that cannot be captured by simple Euclidean geometry, foundational to understanding the 720\textdegree~topology requirement \citep{nolle2012}.}

Nölle et al.\ \citeyearpar{nolle2012} provide a derivation within their proposed H2SI geometry.\footnote{Nölle et al.'s work was presented at DSP 2013 (1--3 July 2013) as peer-reviewed conference proceedings, where they discuss Judd's ``super-importance of hue'' and derive an effective hue-circle circumference of roughly $4\pi$ (720\textdegree) under their proposed metric.} They define a saturation-dependent circumference term $s_2(S)$ for a constant-lightness, constant-saturation hue circle as:

\begin{equation}
    s_2(S) = \sqrt{\frac{5}{2}S}\int_{0}^{2\pi} dH = \pi\sqrt{10}\,S
    \label{eq:hue-circumference}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $S$ denotes saturation in the H2SI construction and $s_2(S)$ is the circumference term (in the authors' metric units) \citep[p.~9, Eq.~(3.10)]{nolle2013h2si_ms}. This expression is the H2SI specialization of the more general hue-arc-length integral implied by Eq.~\ref{eq:line-element}, i.e., integrating the appropriate metric component along a constant-$L$, constant-$S$ hue path. They then compare this circumference to the corresponding radial distance from a saturated point toward the neutral axis, yielding the ratio \citep[p.~9, Eq.~(3.11)]{nolle2013h2si_ms}:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{s_2(S=1)}{s_1} = 4\sqrt{10} \approx 12.65 \approx 4\pi\; (=720^\circ)
    \label{eq:720-result}
\end{equation}

In a Euclidean space, the circumference of a unit circle of radius $r$ is $2\pi r$. Here the derived ratio is approximately $4\pi$, i.e., about $2.01\times 2\pi$, motivating the informal statement that the hue circle behaves as if it had an effective ``circumference'' of roughly 720° rather than 360° \citep[p.~3]{nolle2013h2si_ms}.

\subsection{Physical Interpretation}

What does this mean physically? Consider walking around a hue circle at constant lightness and saturation, taking steps of one just-noticeable difference (JND). In a Euclidean space, you would return to your starting point after $2\pi r$ JND steps, where $r$ is the radius. In the H2SI construction, the corresponding circumference-to-radius ratio is closer to $4\pi$ than $2\pi$, i.e., approximately double in this sense.

This ``extra length'' cannot be represented without distortion in a single global three-dimensional Euclidean coordinate system. It suggests that perceptual color geometry is intrinsically curved, i.e., \emph{non-Euclidean} \citep{nolle2012,roberti2023}. Any attempt to embed color perception in a flat 3D coordinate system must introduce distortions, either uneven discrimination thresholds (as in CIELAB) or hue-dependent perceptual compression (as in HSV/HSL).

\subsection{Implications for Existing Color Spaces}

This result helps explain why even carefully designed spaces like CIELAB exhibit perceptual nonuniformities \citep[p.~530]{kong2021}:\footnote{Kong's dissertation provides comprehensive evidence that CIELAB, while effective for spatial discrimination tasks, fails for temporal color perception: ``CIELAB is not a useful space to predict the perception of dynamic colored light. Today, no color spaces are available that accurately predict the visibility of color differences over time''. For the primary experimental basis of the 10:1 temporal asymmetry, see Sekulovski et al.~\citep[p.~113]{sekulovski2007}.}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CIELAB (1976):} Achieves approximate uniformity in spatial discrimination tasks but performs poorly as a predictor for temporal transitions; Kong argues it is not suitable for dynamic colored light and that no available color space accurately predicts the visibility of temporal differences \citep[p.~3]{kong2021}.
    
    \item \textbf{CAM16-UCS:} Improved spatial uniformity through adaptive chromatic transforms, but computational cost limits real-time use
    
    \item \textbf{OKLab:} Optimized for practical uniformity within the constraint of Euclidean coordinates; excellent for gradients and interpolation
    
    \item \textbf{HSV/HSL:} Euclidean spaces with poor perceptual correspondence; hue angle does not represent equal perceptual steps
\end{itemize}

The engine adopts OKLab as a pragmatic compromise: a space that \emph{minimizes} perceptual distortion while remaining computationally tractable (\S\ref{sec:oklab-implementation}). The 720° result remains relevant for understanding why perfect uniformity is unattainable and why certain design choices (e.g., velocity weights, closure thresholds) require empirical calibration rather than pure geometric derivation.

\subsection{Connection to Möbius Loop Topology}

The 720° result has a surprising implication for loop closure strategies (\S\ref{sec:loop-open}--\ref{sec:loop-phased}). A Möbius strip, a surface with a single half-twist, also requires traversing 720° to return to the original orientation. This coincidence—both involving a 720° return—suggests a potentially useful design heuristic: Möbius-like operations (chromatic inversion with lightness continuity) may provide a compatible loop-closure strategy, though the precise mathematical relationship remains to be formalized.

\emph{Important caveat:} This connection should be understood as a \emph{design heuristic and analogy} rather than a formally derived theorem. The metric circumference result and perceptual nonuniformity are distinct phenomena from orientation reversal in a Möbius strip. The Möbius strategy (\S\ref{sec:loop-mobius}) exploits this parallel as a heuristic to achieve smooth loop closure.

Having established both the Riemannian curvature (\S\ref{sec:riemannian-color}) and the topological impossibility of perfect uniformity (\S\ref{sec:topology-impossibility}), we now face a practical engineering question: which color space provides the best computational approximation? The answer lies in strategic compromise rather than theoretical perfection.

\section{OKLab Color Space}
\label{sec:oklab}
\label{sec:oklab-implementation}

\subsection{Design Rationale}

The selection of a working color space requires balancing competing objectives:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Perceptual accuracy:} Euclidean distances should approximate perceptual differences as closely as possible
    \item \textbf{Computational efficiency:} Transformations must be fast enough for real-time palette generation
    \item \textbf{Numerical stability:} The space should behave well at gamut boundaries and extreme lightness values
    \item \textbf{Industry validation:} The space should have independent verification and adoption
\end{enumerate}

OKLab, introduced by Björn Ottosson in 2020 \citep{ottosson2020}, achieves an excellent balance across these criteria. It has gained rapid adoption in web standards (CSS Color Level 4) \citep{csscolor4}, creative software, and independent validation for gradient quality \citep{levien2021oklab}.

\subsection{Coordinate Representation}

A color in OKLab is represented by three coordinates $(L, a, b)$:

\begin{description}
    \item[$L$ --- Perceived lightness] ranges from 0 (perceptual black) to 1 (brightest white). Equal differences in $L$ correspond to approximately equal perceived brightness changes.
    
    \item[$a$ --- Green–red opponent axis] with positive values indicating reddish tints and negative values indicating greenish tints. Zero is achromatic along this axis.
    
    \item[$b$ --- Blue–yellow opponent axis] with positive values indicating yellowish tints and negative values indicating bluish tints. Zero is achromatic along this axis.
\end{description}

The axes are designed to be \emph{approximately perceptually orthogonal}: altering $L$ alone should not significantly change perceived hue or chroma; altering $a$ or $b$ primarily affects hue and saturation without changing perceived lightness \citep{ottosson2020}.

The perceptual distance between two colors is computed as Euclidean distance:

\begin{equation}
    \Delta E = \sqrt{(L_2 - L_1)^2 + (a_2 - a_1)^2 + (b_2 - b_1)^2}
    \label{eq:oklab-distance}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\Delta E$ denotes color difference in OKLab perceptual space.\footnote{All $\Delta E$ measurements in this specification refer to Euclidean distance in OKLab space unless explicitly noted otherwise. We use ``color difference'' and ``perceptual distance'' interchangeably when referring to this metric.}

\subsection{Transformation Pipeline}

The transformation from sRGB to OKLab follows a fixed matrix pipeline:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Linearize sRGB:} Remove gamma encoding to obtain linear RGB values
    \item \textbf{Linear sRGB to LMS-like:} Apply matrix $M_1$ to map linear sRGB into an LMS-like space (numerically fitted; not identical to physiological cone LMS)
    \item \textbf{Nonlinear compression:} Apply a sign-preserving cube root to obtain $(l', m', s') = (\text{cbrt}(l), \text{cbrt}(m), \text{cbrt}(s))$
    \item \textbf{LMS to Lab:} Apply matrix $M_2$ to obtain final $(L, a, b)$
\end{enumerate}

The transformation matrices are \citep{ottosson2020}\footnote{The OKLab matrix coefficients and transformation pipeline are directly based on Ottosson's C++ reference implementation, available under the MIT license at \url{https://bottosson.github.io/posts/oklab/}. The code was updated 2021-01-25 to use higher-precision sRGB matrices and exactly matching D65 values. For full details and code, see Appendix~\ref{app:oklab-code}.}:

\begin{equation}
    M_1 = \begin{pmatrix}
    0.4122214708 & 0.5363325363 & 0.0514459929 \\
    0.2119034982 & 0.6806995451 & 0.1073969566 \\
    0.0883024619 & 0.2817188376 & 0.6299787005
    \end{pmatrix}
    \label{eq:oklab-m1}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    M_2 = \begin{pmatrix}
    0.2104542553 & 0.7936177850 & -0.0040720468 \\
    1.9779984951 & -2.4285922050 & 0.4505937099 \\
    0.0259040371 & 0.7827717662 & -0.8086757660
    \end{pmatrix}
    \label{eq:oklab-m2}
\end{equation}

These values are standardized in CSS Color Level 4 \citep{csscolor4} and match the reference implementation.

\emph{Implementation note:} Ottosson's C++ code (MIT license) provides the canonical reference for these transformations. Depending on application, it may be preferable to use the sRGB matrices native to your environment for exact consistency. See Appendix~\ref{app:oklab-code} for the full code listing.

Each step models aspects of human vision: $M_1$ maps linear sRGB into an LMS-like response space fitted to color appearance data (so it is cone-inspired rather than a direct physiological LMS model); the cube root models nonlinear perceptual compression—a principle dating to Fechner's seminal psychophysical work (as discussed in \citealt{fairchild2013}, Chapter~3) and refined through modern understanding of cone response functions \citep{ottosson2020}; $M_2$ extracts lightness as a weighted average and opponent-color signals as cone differences.

\subsection{Sign-Preserving Cube Root}

Real implementations can encounter negative LMS values, particularly outside gamut or due to numerical precision at extreme parameter values. The engine uses the \emph{standard sign-preserving cube root} as specified in the OKLab reference implementation: $\text{cbrt}(x) = \text{sgn}(x) \cdot |x|^{1/3}$. This ensures continuity across the achromatic axis and avoids internal inconsistencies when discussing gamut and unbounded behaviour.

\subsection{Computational Efficiency}

The OKLab conversion is computationally lightweight:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Operations:} 18 multiplications, 12 additions, 3 cube roots per conversion
    \item \textbf{Memory:} no heap allocations are required; the transform can be implemented as straight-line arithmetic plus a sign-preserving cube root
    \item \textbf{Parallelisation:} trivially vectorisable with SIMD instructions
    \item \textbf{Performance:} conversion overhead is negligible compared to palette generation logic
\end{itemize}

This efficiency supports real-time palette generation. The forward and inverse pipelines use fixed constant matrices with no iterative solving; any necessary gamut handling is addressed separately (\S\ref{sec:gamut-problem}).

\subsection{Cartesian vs.\ Cylindrical Coordinates (OKLCh)}
\label{sec:oklch}

For certain operations, particularly hue manipulation, a cylindrical form is more convenient. OKLCh represents the same colors using:

\begin{description}
    \item[$L$ --- Lightness] identical to OKLab
    \item[$C$ --- Chroma] defined as $C = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, representing distance from the neutral axis (colorfulness/saturation)
    \item[$h$ --- Hue angle] defined as $h = \operatorname{atan2}(b, a)$, the angle around the $a$–$b$ plane
\end{description}

The conversion is straightforward:
\begin{align}
    C &= \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \label{eq:chroma} \\
    h &= \operatorname{atan2}(b, a) \label{eq:hue-angle}
\end{align}

The engine uses both representations internally:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cartesian (OKLab)} for distance calculations, linear interpolation, and Bézier curve construction
    \item \textbf{Cylindrical (OKLCh)} for hue-related operations such as warmth bias (\S\ref{sec:temperature}), complementary color calculation (\S\ref{sec:loop-mobius}), and palette mode operation (\S\ref{sec:categorical-mode})
\end{itemize}

This separation allows clean reasoning about lightness dynamics (along $L$) versus chromatic dynamics (in the $a$–$b$ plane or around $h$).

\subsection{Implications for Journey Construction}

The choice of OKLab has direct implications for color journey design:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Linear interpolation produces smooth gradients:} A straight line between two colors in OKLab coordinates produces a perceptually smooth transition, unlike RGB, where perceptual step size varies substantially across the interpolation. Figure~\ref{fig:interp-comparison} illustrates this by comparing the perceptual distance between adjacent samples under linear RGB versus linear OKLab interpolation. The key failure mode is that linear RGB paths can traverse the achromatic region, producing grey midpoints and uneven perceptual steps, which motivates expressing constraints in OKLab units and enforcing them per step in the small-step regime.
    
    \item \textbf{Distance calculations are meaningful:} Constraints like $\Delta_{\min}$ and $\Delta_{\max}$ correspond to actual perceived differences, enabling principled constraint enforcement.
    
    \item \textbf{Dynamics are separable:} The orthogonality of $L$, $a$, $b$ means lightness can be adjusted independently of chromaticity, simplifying multi-objective optimization.
    
    \item \textbf{Hue interpolation is stable:} Unlike HSV, where interpolating across the 0°/360° boundary causes discontinuities, OKLab's Cartesian representation avoids such artifacts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Temporal vs.\ Spatial Color Perception}
\label{sec:temporal-spatial}

\subsection{Eye Movement and Chromatic Sensitivity}

Human color perception differs fundamentally between static spatial patterns and dynamic temporal transitions. Braun et al.\ \citeyearpar{braun2017} measured chromatic sensitivity during different types of eye movements:

\begin{description}
    \item[Smooth pursuit:] When tracking a moving object with smooth eye movements, chromatic sensitivity \emph{increases} by approximately 12\% \citep{braun2017}.\footnote{These percentages are stated in the abstract of \citet{braun2017}.} The visual system enhances color discrimination during continuous tracking.
    
    \item[Fixation:] During stable fixation, chromatic sensitivity remains at baseline levels.
    
    \item[Saccadic suppression:] During rapid eye movements (saccades), chromatic sensitivity \emph{decreases} by approximately 58\% \citep{braun2017}. The visual system suppresses color information during ballistic gaze shifts to avoid motion blur.
\end{description}

This asymmetry has direct implications for color journey design: transitions should be optimized for \emph{smooth viewing} rather than rapid scanning. Gradual, continuous color changes exploit the enhanced chromatic sensitivity during pursuit, while abrupt changes risk perceptual suppression.

\subsection{Temporal Asymmetry in Color Channels}

Beyond eye movement effects, the visual system exhibits channel-specific temporal sensitivities. Sekulovski et al.\ \citeyearpar{sekulovski2007} conducted a series of psychophysical experiments to quantify these differences, using a classical method to measure discrete color difference thresholds.\footnote{Sekulovski's research at Philips Research introduced the Delta-E-ab per second metric for temporal color transitions, establishing that smoothness thresholds are approximately 10 times smaller for luminance than for chroma/hue changes in CIELAB.}

\subsubsection*{Experimental methodology}

Sekulovski's team presented observers with discrete linear temporal color transitions in CIE LCh space, varying independently along three axes: lightness ($L$), chroma ($C$), and hue ($h$). For each condition, they measured the threshold step size (expressed in $\Delta E_{ab}$ units) at which observers could still perceive the transition as perceptually smooth. They systematically varied:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transition direction:} Three independent axes (lightness, chroma, hue)
    \item \textbf{Frequency:} Multiple frame rates (5, 10, 20, 30, and 40 Hz)
    \item \textbf{Base color:} Multiple starting colors with different chromaticity coordinates
\end{itemize}

Thresholds were analyzed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with direction of change, frequency, and base color as fixed factors, and participant as a random factor \citep[pp.~113--114]{sekulovski2007}.

\subsubsection*{The 10:1 ratio: statistical findings}

The ANOVA revealed a highly significant main effect of direction of change ($p < 0.01$) \citep[p.~113]{sekulovski2007}. Post hoc tests demonstrated that mean threshold values for lightness changes were approximately \textbf{10 times smaller} than those for chroma and hue changes \citep[p.~113]{sekulovski2007}. Quantitatively, this implies that to maintain comparable perceived smoothness, allowable step sizes in lightness should be about \emph{one tenth} the size of step sizes in chroma or hue (in $\Delta E_{ab}$ units), reflecting a strong temporal asymmetry across channels.

Kong's later dissertation \citep[p.~3]{kong2021} summarizes this result, describing the smoothness visibility threshold as about ten times smaller for lightness changes than for chroma or hue changes in CIELAB.

Read operationally, the result indicates that lightness changes must be constrained much more tightly than chromatic changes for transitions to remain perceptually smooth.

In other words, observers are approximately \textbf{10 times more sensitive} to temporal changes in lightness than to changes in chromatic attributes. This 10:1 asymmetry is empirically robust, validated across multiple base colors and viewing frequencies by controlled experimental design and statistical analysis \citep[pp.~113--114]{sekulovski2007}.

\subsubsection*{Independence across base colors}

Crucially, Sekulovski's ANOVA also revealed that lightness thresholds showed \emph{no significant interaction} with base color point, whereas chroma and hue thresholds did vary with base color \citep[p.~113]{sekulovski2007}. This finding is expressed as:

\begin{quote}
``No main effect of the base color point on the thresholds for lightness changes was found for the chromatic color points\ldots\ This shows independence of the thresholds to changes in chromaticity and dependence on lightness'' \citep[p.~113]{sekulovski2007}.
\end{quote}

This independence across base colors is critical for color journey design: a single 10:1 temporal sensitivity weighting can be applied globally, rather than requiring color-dependent corrections. Within the tested conditions, the ratio appears stable across base colors, and we adopt it as a working default while retaining calibration as future work.

\subsection{Design Implications}

We model temporal smoothness as a weighted path-length constraint in OKLab/OKLCh, where weights approximate relative JND sensitivity per channel under transition viewing. The channel weights are chosen so that visually dominant changes (notably in lightness) are penalised more strongly than less salient changes.
As Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-weights} summarizes, we implement this as a weighted path-length constraint in OKLab/OKLCh space.

These findings directly inform the engine's velocity weighting scheme (\S\ref{sec:perceptual-velocity}). Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-weights} visualizes the intuition: small changes in lightness are typically more salient over time than comparable chromatic changes, motivating a substantially higher lightness weight.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Lightness velocity weight:} $w_L = 10.0$ reflects the heightened temporal sensitivity \citep{sekulovski2007}
    \item \textbf{Chroma velocity weight:} $w_C = 1.0$ serves as the baseline reference
    \item \textbf{Hue velocity weight:} $w_H \approx 1.5$ to $2.0$ is a design parameter requiring empirical validation
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Justification for hue weight range}

We treat $w_H$ as a tunable parameter and adopt a conservative range ($\approx 1.5$--$2.0$) rather than a single fixed value for three reasons. First, classic arguments about the ``super-importance of hue'' suggest that hue can contribute disproportionately to perceived difference around the chromatic circle relative to a purely Euclidean embedding \citep{judd1940,nolle2012}. Second, hue changes often correspond to a perceptually categorical change (e.g., ``bluish'' to ``greenish'') even when chroma is held constant, so moderate weighting helps prevent visually abrupt phase shifts in temporal transitions. Third, existing temporal threshold studies support the strong $w_L:w_C$ asymmetry but do not provide an equally precise statistical separation between hue and chroma, so we avoid overclaiming and frame $w_H$ as an empirically calibratable design choice.

The 10:1 ratio for $w_L : w_C$ is empirically validated through Sekulovski's controlled psychophysical measurement and ANOVA analysis \citep[pp.~113--114]{sekulovski2007}. However, hue velocity weighting remains a \emph{design heuristic}; Sekulovski's data show hue and chroma thresholds are comparable in magnitude but do not provide a statistically precise ratio distinguishing them. Future work should employ psychometric methods (e.g., two-alternative forced-choice paradigms \citep{wang2022}) to measure hue temporal sensitivity rigorously and establish whether a hue-specific weighting factor can be justified beyond the baseline chroma sensitivity.

\section{Summary: Perceptual Foundations Determine Engineering Constraints}
\label{sec:perceptual-summary}

The perceptual foundations established in this section impose four practical constraints on color journey construction:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Work in perceptually uniform space:} The Riemannian structure (\S\ref{sec:riemannian-color}) requires a coordinate system where Euclidean interpolation approximates perceptual geodesics. OKLab provides this approximation with minimal computational overhead (\S\ref{sec:oklab-implementation}).
    
    \item \textbf{Accept imperfect uniformity:} The practical impossibility of perfect perceptual uniformity (\S\ref{sec:topology-impossibility}) means all design parameters must be empirically calibrated rather than geometrically derived. The 720\textdegree\ result helps explain why certain operations, particularly loop closure, benefit from heuristic strategies.
    
    \item \textbf{Weight lightness changes heavily:} The 10:1 temporal sensitivity ratio (\S\ref{sec:temporal-spatial}) requires lightness velocity to dominate perceptual speed calculations. Lightness changes must be controlled more tightly than chromatic changes to maintain perceived smoothness.
    
    \item \textbf{Optimize for smooth viewing:} Enhanced chromatic sensitivity during pursuit eye movements (\S\ref{sec:temporal-spatial}) favors gradual, continuous transitions over abrupt changes. The engine prioritizes perceptual continuity over geometric optimality.
\end{enumerate}

Where perceptual science remains unsettled (for example, suprathreshold distance additivity and hue-specific temporal weighting), we treat current choices as principled defaults and explicitly welcome future empirical updates.

\subsection{Implications for Journey Construction}

These perceptual constraints translate directly into algorithmic requirements:

\begin{description}
    \item[Tunable parameters:] Minimum step size $\Delta_{\min}$, maximum step size $\Delta_{\max}$, channel weights $(w_L, w_C, w_H)$, and transition scaling factors all operate in OKLab perceptual units.
    
    \item[Calibration strategy:] JND thresholds, temporal sensitivity ratios, and domain-specific viewing conditions determine default parameter values. User preferences can override defaults while maintaining perceptual validity.
    
    \item[Geometric machinery:] The Riemannian structure justifies using Bézier curves, spline interpolation, and geodesic approximations in OKLab space (\S\ref{sec:bezier}). Straight lines in OKLab approximate perceptually smooth paths.
    
    \item[Gamut management:] Position-dependent metric tensors inform gamut mapping strategies: projection toward the achromatic center follows the intrinsic geometry revealed by discrimination threshold ellipses (\S\ref{sec:gamut-correction}).
\end{description}

With these perceptual foundations established, we now turn to the geometric and algorithmic machinery for constructing color paths that respect these constraints. Section~\ref{sec:journey-metaphor} develops the computational framework for generating perceptually smooth color progressions within the perceptual space defined here.
